In the game, damaging the enemy robot involves hitting their armor plates to drain their HP. As such, one of the most important tasks for our robot is robustly detecting armor plates in real time. We originally tried to use OpenCV's color based detection to identify the plates, but often had misidentifications.

As such, we pivoted to a deep learning approach, collecting our own dataset to train a model.

### Dataset Collection

Since there were no previous datasets collected for armor plates specifically for the Arc Robotics competition, we had to collect our own. We split the dataset into two: one for identifying plates in an image, and one for classifying the plate's icon.

Our plate detection dataset was created by taking photos of our robots with plates attached in different scenarios. We ran into an initial problem of overfitting to black blobs since all our robots were black. We added a supplementary dataset of just floating plates which eliminated the bias.

The icon dataset was generated by using a trained plate detection model, and then grayscaling everything to make it more uniform.

### Labeling and Autolabeling

We used LabelStudio as our labeling software, and SAM3 with hand-crafted prompts to help auto-label the data, drastically speeding up our workflow, of course with human verification to make sure the labels were correct. We ended up with around 750 images for the plate dataset, and around 1,000 images for the icon dataset.

### Model Selection and Training

Our hardware is a Jetson Orin AGX, with a pair of Basler cameras. Since these cameras can go up to 160 fps+, we wanted our whole pipeline to be able to reach at least 120 fps. Because of this, we had a strong priority on inference speed.

- **Plate Detection**: We went with Ultralytics' yolo26 nano model, seeing that TensorRT optimizations on a T4 chip brought inference down to around 3ms on their website.
- **Icon Classification**: We ended up going with a small custom CNN with 7 layers. We evaluated Ultralytics' yolo26 nano classifier as well but found that it was much larger than what was needed for simple classifying, as such we went with the custom CNN.

We trained the models on Google Colab, using Comet as a visualizer to check training and finetuning parameters as needed.

### Implementation

After training the models, we exported them as `.engine` files through TensorRT with half precision for more speed. Additionally since we will often see more than 1 plate in an image, we made sure to batch our icon classifier to be able to classify multiple plates at once.
